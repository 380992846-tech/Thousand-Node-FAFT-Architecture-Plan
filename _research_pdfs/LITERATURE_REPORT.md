### A1. Ongaro, Ousterhout | "In Search of an Understandable Consensus Algorithm" | USENIX ATC 2014

- §8 "Client interaction" gives **both** read mechanisms in one passage. Exact quote:
  > "Raft handles this by having the leader exchange heartbeat messages with a majority of the cluster before responding to read-only requests. Alternatively, the leader could rely on the heartbeat mechanism to provide a form of lease [9], but this would rely on timing for safety (it assumes bounded clock skew)."
- Precondition (exact quote): *"The Leader Completeness Property guarantees that a leader has all committed entries, but at the start of its term, it may not know which those are. To find out, it needs to commit an entry from its term. Raft handles this by having each leader commit a blank no-op entry into the log at the start of its term."*

### A2. Diego Ongaro | "Consensus: Bridging Theory and Practice" (PhD dissertation) | Stanford University, August 2014
- URL (verified live, 258 pages, HTTP 200): https://web.stanford.edu/~ouster/cgi-bin/papers/OngaroPhD.pdf
  - Stanford purl cited in the document itself: http://purl.stanford.edu/qr033xr6097
- §6.4 (pp. 72–73) "Processing read-only queries more efficiently". The mechanism is a **5-step procedure**; the name `readIndex` is an ordinary local variable, not a branded mechanism. Exact quotes:
  - Step 1: *"If the leader has not yet marked an entry from its current term committed, it waits until it has done so. The Leader Completeness Property guarantees that a leader has all committed entries, but at the start of its term, it may not know which those are. To find out, it needs to commit an entry from its term. Raft handles this by having each leader commit a blank no-op entry into the log at the start of its term. As soon as this no-op entry is committed, the leader's commit index will be at least as large as any other servers' during its term."*
  - Step 2: *"The leader saves its current commit index in a local variable readIndex. This will be used as a lower bound for the version of the state that the query operates against."*
  - Step 3: *"The leader needs to make sure it hasn't been superseded by a newer leader of which it is unaware. It issues a new round of heartbeats and waits for their acknowledgments from a majority of the cluster. Once these acknowledgments are received, the leader knows that there could not have existed a leader for a greater term at the moment it sent the heartbeats. Thus, the readIndex was, at the time, the largest commit index ever seen by any server in the cluster."*
  - Step 4: *"The leader waits for its state machine to advance at least as far as the readIndex; this is current enough to satisfy linearizability."*
  - Step 5: *"Finally, the leader issues the query against its state machine and replies to the client with the results."*
- **Round of communication (exact quote):** *"To improve efficiency further, the leader can amortize the cost of confirming its leadership: it can use a single round of heartbeats for any number of read-only queries that it has accumulated."* → **1 extra RTT per batch**, amortized; plus a one-time cost of committing a no-op entry in the leader's term (itself ~1 RTT of AppendEntries).
- **What it assumes (exact quote):** §6.4.1 opens *"Up until now, the approach to read-only queries presented has provided linearizability in an asynchronous model (where clocks, processors, and messages can all operate at arbitrary speeds)."* → **no clock assumption at all**.
- **Follower reads** are described but explicitly not implemented (exact quote): *"To serve reads safely, the follower could issue a request to the leader that just asked for a current readIndex (the leader would execute steps 1–3 above); the follower could then execute steps 4 and 5 on its own state machine for any number of accumulated read-only queries. ... Followers in LogCabin do not currently serve read-only requests."*
- **Leader-lease variant §6.4.1 (exact quotes):** lease length = election timeout divided by a drift bound: *"Once the leader's heartbeats were acknowledged by a majority of the cluster, the leader would assume that no other server will become leader for about an election timeout, and it could extend its lease accordingly"* (Figure 6.3 caption: lease extends to `start + election timeout / clock drift bound`). Assumption: *"The lease approach assumes a bound on clock drift across servers (over a given time period, no server's clock increases more than this bound times any other). Discovering and maintaining this bound might present operational challenges (e.g., due to scheduling and garbage collection pauses, virtual machine migrations, or clock rate adjustments for time synchronization). If the assumptions are violated, the system could return arbitrarily stale information."* The author **does not recommend it**: *"LogCabin does not currently implement this alternative, and we do not recommend using it unless necessary to meet performance requirements."*
- The dissertation also states the two stale-read bugs found in third-party Raft implementations: *"Problems due to stale reads have already been discovered in two third-party Raft implementations [45], so this issue deserves careful attention."*

### A3. Iulian Moraru, David G. Andersen, Michael Kaminsky | "Paxos Quorum Leases: Fast Reads Without Sacrificing Writes" | Proc. 5th ACM Symposium on Cloud Computing (SoCC), Seattle WA, November 2014; DOI 10.1145/2670979.2671001
- URL (author homepage, abstract + BibTeX verified): https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~dga/papers/leases-socc2014-abstract.html
- PDF: https://www.cs.cmu.edu/~dga/papers/leases-socc2014.pdf
- **Safety argument / assumptions (§3.1 "Assumptions", exact quote):** *"Communication between the nodes is asynchronous: messages may be lost or delayed indefinitely. Replicas do not synchronize clocks, but their clock rates are assumed to be similar, such that a modest guard time can account for clock drift over a short interval. Failures are non-Byzantine: replicas can crash or fail to respond indefinitely, but they will not take actions that do not conform to the protocol."*
- Also (§3.4, exact quote): *"To avoid a dependency on external clock synchronization, we set lease timers based upon the causal ordering of messaging events using a lightweight peer-to-peer protocol"* — with a *t_guard* plus *t_lease*.
- **Latency:** a replica holding a lease reads locally with **0 RTT**. Evaluation: *"over 80% of reads are handled locally"*.
- **Exact safety claim (§3.6, verbatim):** *"In conclusion, a Paxos system that implements quorum read leases ensures strict serializability."*
- **Exact unavailability bound (§3.8, verbatim):** *"The maximum period of write unavailability caused by a failed lease holder is therefore t_grace + t_wait = t_grace + t_guard + t_lease."* Their measured setting: *"the lease duration was 2 seconds, every grantor renewed the current lease after 500 milliseconds"*; local experiment used a 5 s grace period (so 5 + 2 + 2 = 9 s), matching the paper's *"approximately 9 seconds for 2 seconds lease and grant"*.
- Also verified, showing leases are a *performance* not *safety* prerequisite: *"With quorum leases, different objects may be granted to different quorums... the changes to [the system] are critical for safety, only for performance"* (the paper's wording at that point is about implementation changes; the load-bearing safety statement is the §3.6 sentence quoted above).

### A4. A. Jesse Jiryu Davis, Murat Demirbas, Lingzhi Deng | "LeaseGuard: Raft Leases Done Right" | arXiv:2512.15659 (v2, 19 Dec 2025); journal reference: Proc. ACM Manag. Data 4, 1 (SIGMOD), Article 49 (April 2026), 25 pages; DOI 10.1145/3786663
- URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.15659 ; HTML: https://arxiv.org/html/2512.15659v2 ; PDF: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.15659v2
- **Verified: this is the "LeaseGuard" you asked about.** Title, authors, venue and year confirmed from the arXiv abstract page.
- **Latency claim (abstract, verbatim):** *"By replacing LogCabin's default consistency mechanism (quorum checks), LeaseGuard reduces the overhead of consistent reads from one to zero network roundtrips."*
- **Clock assumption (verified, two distinct levels — this is the important nuance):**
  - §5.3 heading: *"Leases without bounded-uncertainty clocks"*. Verbatim: *"Most of LeaseGuard can be implemented using local timers with bounded drift rates; bounded-uncertainty clocks are not required. All nodes must know some value ϵ, the maximum amount that their clocks can gain or lose while measuring a duration of Δ."* The revised rules: *"a leader can advance its commitIndex or serve reads so long as its last entry in any previous term is > Δ + ϵ old, and the last committed entry in its own term is < Δ − ϵ old."*
  - But: *"Timers suffice for LeaseGuard with deferred commit writes, but not for inherited lease reads."* §4.3 "Correctness with clock uncertainty" states *"Inherited lease reads require correct clock bounds!"* and §5.3 concludes *"Thus, systems must have access to clocks with bounded uncertainty to implement inherited lease reads."*
  - The failure mode if bounds are wrong (§4.3, verbatim): *"If a clock's bounds are wrong, i.e. if the true time is outside the interval [earliest, latest] ... then multiple leaders may think they are leaseholders at once. In that case a deposed leader may execute reads while a higher-term leader commits writes. Reads from the deposed leader will violate Read-Your-Writes, and thus violate linearizability."*
- **Lease duration guidance (§5.2, verbatim):** *"LeaseGuard allows the election timeout ET and lease duration Δ to be tuned independently. If ET is a fixed number, it is best to set Δ = ET"* (Figure 5 caption: *"Election timeout = Δ is usually optimal."*)
- Uses TrueTime-style bounded-uncertainty clocks; names Amazon's `clock-bound` daemon and cites *"an average clock error less than 50µs"* in their evaluation environment.

### A5. PingCAP | "Follower Read" (TiDB documentation) | TiDB docs (stable), accessed for this report
- URL: https://docs.pingcap.com/tidb/stable/follower-read/ ; source mirror: https://raw.githubusercontent.com/pingcap/docs/master/follower-read.md
- **Exact mechanism, verbatim:** *"To allow data reading from the follower node without violating linearizability or affecting Snapshot Isolation in TiDB, the follower node needs to use `ReadIndex` of the Raft protocol to ensure that the read request can read the latest data that has been committed on the leader node."*
- **Exact procedure, verbatim:** *"When the follower node processes a read request, it first uses `ReadIndex` of the Raft protocol to interact with the leader of the Region, to obtain the latest commit index of the current Raft group. After the latest commit index of the leader is applied locally to the follower, the processing of a read request starts."*
- **Cost, verbatim:** *"To ensure strong consistency of the read results, Follower Read communicates with the leader before reading to confirm the latest commit progress (by executing the Raft `ReadIndex` operation). This introduces an additional network interaction."* → **1 extra RTT (follower → leader → follower).** Note that here `ReadIndex` **is** used as a proper noun — the coinage comes from the etcd/TiKV line of work, not from Ongaro.
- **Clock assumption: NONE** (pure message round; logical TSO timestamps).

### A6. PingCAP | "Understanding Stale Read and safe-ts in TiKV" | TiDB docs (stable), accessed for this report
- URL: https://docs.pingcap.com/tidb/stable/troubleshoot-stale-read/ ; source mirror: https://raw.githubusercontent.com/pingcap/docs/master/troubleshoot-stale-read.md
- **Exact definitions (verbatim):**
  - *"The safe-ts is a timestamp that each peer in a Region maintains. It ensures that all transactions with a timestamp less than this value have been applied locally, which enables local Stale Read."*
  - *"The resolved-ts is a timestamp that guarantees all transactions with a timestamp less than this value have been applied by the leader. Unlike safe-ts, which is a peer concept, resolved-ts is only maintained by the Region leader. Followers might have a smaller apply index than the leader, so resolved-ts cannot be directly treated as safe-ts in followers."*
  - *"TiKV implements this safety guarantee by ensuring that safe-ts is always less than or equal to resolved-ts."*
- **Key negative finding for your question about clock drift:** TiKV's bounded staleness (**0 RTT**) is *not* bounded by a wall-clock drift assumption on the follower. The bound is a **logical-timestamp bound**: the leader pushes `resolved-ts` (derived from the PD/TSO timestamps of locks and transactions) plus a validating apply index to followers over the `CheckLeader` RPC, and a follower's `safe-ts` only advances once it has applied up to that index. The cost is staleness induced by *replication lag and lock/intent age*, not clock error. **TiKV documentation states no ε / bounded-drift assumption for safe-ts** — I found none.

### A7. Cockroach Labs | "Follower Reads" (CockroachDB documentation) | CockroachDB docs (stable), accessed for this report
- URL: https://docs.cockroachlabs.com/docs/stable/follower-reads (markdown: append `.md`)
- **Mechanism (verbatim):** *"Each CockroachDB range tracks a property called its closed timestamp, which means that no new writes can ever be introduced at or below that timestamp. ... With follower reads, any replica in a range can serve a read for a key as long as the time at which the operation is performed (i.e., the AS OF SYSTEM TIME value) is less than or equal to the range's closed timestamp."*
- **Latency:** 0 RTT for the read itself (served by the nearest replica).
- **Exact staleness-limitation language (verbatim):** *"Can tolerate reading older data (at least 4.2 seconds in the past) ..."* for exact-staleness reads; bounded staleness reads use `with_max_staleness(INTERVAL)` / `with_min_timestamp(...)`.
- **Availability claim (verbatim):** *"Once a replica begins serving follower reads at a timestamp, it will always continue to serve follower reads at that timestamp. Even if the replica becomes completely partitioned away from the rest of its range, it will continue to stay available for (increasingly) stale reads."*

### A8. Cockroach Labs | "Transaction Layer" — closed timestamps and max clock offset enforcement | CockroachDB docs (stable), accessed for this report
- URL: https://docs.cockroachlabs.com/docs/stable/architecture/transaction-layer
- **The clock assumption, exact quote:** *"CockroachDB requires moderate levels of clock synchronization to preserve data consistency. For this reason, when a node detects that its clock is out of sync with at least half of the other nodes in the cluster by 80% of the maximum offset allowed, it crashes immediately."*
- **And the safety caveat, verbatim:** *"While serializable consistency is maintained under SERIALIZABLE isolation regardless of clock skew, skew outside the configured clock offset bounds can result in violations of single-key linearizability between causally dependent transactions."*
- **Closed-timestamp mechanism, verbatim:** *"In other words, a closed timestamp is a promise by the range's leaseholder to its follower replicas that it will not accept writes below that timestamp. Generally speaking, the leaseholder continuously closes timestamps a few seconds in the past. ... This means that a follower replica can start serving reads with timestamps at or below the closed timestamp as soon as it has applied all of the Raft commands up to the position in the Raft log specified by the leaseholder."*
- So: CockroachDB follower reads = **0 RTT**, bounded staleness, resting on an HLC whose drift is **enforced** (crash-on-violation) rather than assumed. The `--max-offset` default is 500 ms per the `cockroach start` flag documentation (I read the enforcement text; the 500 ms default value itself I did not re-verify in this session — treat the exact default as **unverified**).

### A9. Robert Lyerly (Meta), Scott Pruett, Kevin Doherty, Greg Rogers, Nathan Bronson, John Hugg | "Skybridge: Bounded Staleness for Distributed Caches" | 19th USENIX OSDI, July 7–9 2025, Boston MA; ISBN 978-1-939133-47-2
- URL: https://www.usenix.org/system/files/osdi25-lyerly.pdf ; presentation page: https://www.usenix.org/conference/osdi25/presentation/lyerly
- **Best formal definition of bounded staleness I found (verbatim):** *"Bounded staleness [40] is a read consistency level exposed by asynchronously-replicated datastores. Unlike eventual consistency, which specifies that reads to the datastore will observe a write at some point without any conditions on timeliness, bounded staleness guarantees that reads will observe any write within some system-provided time bound. Concretely, if write W_T is performed at time T and the system provides staleness bound S, then any read R performed at or after T + S is guaranteed to observe W_T regardless of whether the read is to the primary or a replica. Note that under bounded staleness the reader is allowed to observe subsequent writes W_{T+1}, W_{T+2} ... (i.e., fresher data) but reads must reflect all older writes W_{T−1}, ... that preceded W_T."*
- **The clock-drift bound, exactly as they express it (verbatim):** *"1. If Time_now − (S − ε) < max(HLC_wm, HLC_item) (where ε is max NTP clock skew) then the cache item is fresh"* — with footnote 1: *"If maximum clock skew exceeds ε, TAO may silently violate bounded staleness and return stale data to users."*
- **Operational ε (verbatim):** *"Meta's hosts use NTP to synchronize physical clocks. NTP is known to have on the order of hundreds of milliseconds of clock skew. Meta is investigating using more precise clock synchronization such as PTP, but for now we move the staleness bound forward by 50ms to try to account for clock skew."*
- Default bound S = 2 seconds; *"Skybridge's ability to provide 2-second bounded staleness for 99.99998%"* of fail-closed reads.
- Also uses **leases** (the "Skylease" component) — a concrete instance of leases being used to make a *staleness/time* bound safe, not to make reads linearizable.

### A10. James C. Corbett et al. (Google) | "Spanner: Google's Globally-Distributed Database" | 10th USENIX OSDI 2012
- URL: https://static.googleusercontent.com/media/research.google.com/en//archive/spanner-osdi2012.pdf
- **TrueTime = the canonical "uncertainty interval" assumption (verbatim):** *"TrueTime explicitly represents time as a TTinterval, which is an interval with bounded time uncertainty (unlike standard time interfaces that give clients no notion of uncertainty). The endpoints of a TTinterval are of type TTstamp. The TT.now() method returns a TTinterval that is guaranteed to contain the absolute time during which TT.now() was invoked. ... Define the instantaneous error bound as ε, which is half of the interval's width, and the average error bound as ε̅."*
- **How ε is obtained (verbatim):** *"Between synchronizations, a daemon advertises a slowly increasing time uncertainty. ε is derived from conservatively applied worst-case local clock drift. ε also depends on time-master uncertainty and communication delay to the time masters. In our production environment, ε is typically a sawtooth function of time, varying from about 1 to 7 ms over each poll interval. ... The daemon's poll interval is currently 30 seconds, and the current applied drift rate is set at 200 microseconds/second."*
- Relevant to Topic A #4: Spanner's read-only transactions are lock-free and can be served by *any* replica whose state is up to date enough; the safety argument is rooted in `ε`, not in message rounds.

| TiDB/TiKV **Follower Read** (strong) | **None** (logical TSO + `ReadIndex`) | **1 RTT** follower→leader for the commit index | A5 |
| TiKV **Stale Read** (`safe-ts` ≤ `resolved-ts`) | **None stated** — bound comes from timestamp oracle + apply index, not from wall-clock drift | **0 RTT**; staleness bounded by replication lag / intent age | A6 |

| Spanner snapshot / read-only txn | **TrueTime-style uncertainty interval** with ε (half-width), ε from worst-case drift (200 µs/s) + master uncertainty + comm delay | Lock-free, any sufficiently up-to-date replica; commit-wait ≈ 2ε | A10 |

**Bottom line for A4:** the only mechanisms with a genuine *zero-clock-assumption* linearizable read are the ReadIndex heartbeat round (1 RTT) and quorum/leader leases that are validated causally rather than by wall clock. Every **0 RTT** mechanism either (a) relaxes linearizability to bounded staleness (TiKV `safe-ts`, CockroachDB closed timestamps, Skybridge), or (b) pays for 0 RTT with a **clock** assumption (Raft leases, LeaseGuard inherited leases, TrueTime).

## TOPIC B — Failure detector theory

### B1. Tushar Deepak Chandra, Sam Toueg | "Unreliable Failure Detectors for Reliable Distributed Systems" | Journal of the ACM 43(2): 225–267, March 1996; DOI 10.1145/226643.226647
- Page range **verified twice**: (i) the scanned JACM PDF's own running text contains the string `Journal    pp 225-267`, and the PDF has exactly 43 pages (= 267 − 225 + 1); (ii) DBLP mirror: https://sigmod.org/publications/dblp/db/journals/jacm/ChandraT96.html
- PDF (OCR scan of the JACM article; fetchable, HTTP 200): https://raw.githubusercontent.com/davideuler/architecture.of.internet-product/6473bc0e6a2d55b8f69348ef089daaeef4ddd5a9/9.50-favorite-technical-papers/Unreliable%20Failure%20Detectors%20for%20Reliable%20Distributed%20Systems-p225-chandra.pdf
- **§2.3 Completeness (verbatim):**
  - *"Strong Completeness. Eventually every process that crashes is permanently suspected by every correct process."*
  - *"Weak Completeness. Eventually every process that crashes is permanently suspected by some correct process."*
- **§2.3 Accuracy (verbatim):**
  - *"Strong Accuracy. No process is suspected before it crashes."*
  - *"Weak Accuracy. Some correct process is never suspected."*
  - The paper's own framing: *"However, completeness by itself is not a useful property. To see this, consider a failure detector which causes every process to permanently suspect every other process in the system. Such a failure detector trivially satisfies strong completeness but is clearly useless..."*
- **Eventual accuracy (verbatim):**
  - *"Eventual Strong Accuracy. There is a time after which correct processes are not suspected by any correct process."*
  - *"Eventual Weak Accuracy. There is a time after which some correct process is never suspected by any correct process."*
  - *"We will refer to eventual strong accuracy and eventual weak accuracy as eventual accuracy properties, and strong accuracy and weak accuracy as perpetual accuracy properties."*
- **§2.4 The eight classes (verbatim):** *"A failure detector is said to be Perfect if it satisfies strong completeness and strong accuracy. The set of all such failure detectors, called the class of Perfect failure detectors, is denoted by 𝒫. Similar definitions arise for each pair of completeness and accuracy properties. There are eight such pairs, obtained by selecting one of the two completeness properties and one of the four accuracy properties introduced in the previous section. The resulting definitions and corresponding notation are given in Figure 1."*
- **⚠️ Precision finding you should not gloss over.** I rendered Figure 1 (JACM p. 234) at 600 dpi and read the table directly. It contains **only six names**; two cells have **symbols and no name**:

  | Completeness ↓ / Accuracy → | Strong | Weak | Eventual Strong | Eventual Weak |
  |---|---|---|---|---|
  | **Strong** | Perfect 𝒫 | Strong 𝒮 | Eventually Perfect ◇𝒫 | Eventually Strong ◇𝒮 |
  | **Weak** | *(no name)* 𝒬 | Weak 𝒲 | *(no name)* ◇𝒬 | Eventually Weak ◇𝒲 |

  A full-text grep for `Quasi`/`quasi` over the OCR'd paper returns **zero** matches. So the conventional labels **"Quasi-Perfect" and "Eventually Quasi-Perfect" are NOT in CT96** — they are later community coinages for the 𝒬 and ◇𝒬 cells.
- **◇W is the paper's own weakest class (verbatim, §1):** *"Of special interest is ◇W, the weakest class of failure detectors considered in this paper. Informally, a failure detector is in ◇W if it satisfies the following two properties: Completeness. There is a time after which every process that crashes is permanently suspected by some correct process. Accuracy. There is a time after which some correct process is never suspected by any correct process."* And: *"Such a failure detector can make an infinite number of mistakes: Each local failure detector module can repeatedly add and then remove correct processes from its list of suspects ... Moreover, some correct processes may be erroneously suspected to have crashed by all the other processes throughout the entire execution."*
- **Fault-tolerance lower bound (verbatim, Theorem 6.3.1):** *"Consensus cannot be solved using ◇𝒫 in asynchronous systems with f ≥ ⌈n/2⌉."* Consequence stated in §6.3: *"with failure detectors with eventual accuracy (i.e., in ◇𝒫, ◇𝒬, ◇𝒮, or ◇𝒲), our Consensus algorithms require a majority of the processes to be correct. It turns out that this requirement is necessary."*
- **The FD ↔ partial-synchrony bridge you asked for (Theorem 9.1, verbatim):** *"Consider a partially synchronous system S that conforms to 𝒮3, that is, for every run of S there is a Global Stabilization Time (GST) after which some bounds on relative process speeds and message transmission times hold (the values of GST and these bounds are not known). The algorithm in Figure 10 implements an Eventually Perfect failure detector ◇𝒫 in S."* The algorithm (verbatim): *"To measure elapsed time, each process p maintains a local clock, say by counting the number of steps that it takes. Each process p periodically sends a 'p-is-alive' message to all the processes. If p does not receive a 'q-is-alive' message from some process q for Δp(q) time units on its clock, p adds q to its list of suspects. If p receives 'q-is-alive' from some process q that it currently suspects, p knows that its previous time-out on q was premature. In this case, p removes q from its list of suspects and increases its time-out period Δp(q)."* And on the models (verbatim): *"Dwork et al. [1988] consider two models of partial synchrony. Roughly speaking, the first model (denoted 𝒮1 here) stipulates that in every execution there are bounds on relative process speeds and on message transmission times, but these bounds are not known. In the second model (denoted 𝒮2) these bounds are known, but they hold only after some unknown time (called GST for Global Stabilization Time). In each one of these two models (with crash failures), it is easy to implement an Eventually Perfect failure detector ◇𝒫 ∈ 𝒫? in fact, we can implement such a failure detector in a weaker model of partial synchrony (denoted 𝒮3): one in which bounds exist but they are not known and they hold only after some unknown time GST."*
- **"Failure detectors can be viewed as a more abstract and modular way of..."** (§9, immediately following Theorem 9.1) — the paper's own statement of the reduction from synchrony assumptions to FD properties.

### B2. Tushar Deepak Chandra, Vassos Hadzilacos, Sam Toueg | "The Weakest Failure Detector for Solving Consensus" | Journal of the ACM 43(4): 685–722, July 1996; DOI 10.1145/234533.234549
- Pages verified: DBLP mirror https://sigmod.org/publications/dblp/db/journals/jacm/ChandraHT96.html → `J. ACM 43(4): 685-722(1996)`. (dl.acm.org 403s, as you predicted; the DBLP mirror is authoritative and fetchable.)
- **Exact theorem, from the authors' own abstract page** (Vassos Hadzilacos, University of Toronto, author homepage — HTTP 200): http://www.cs.utoronto.ca/~vassos/research/publications/CHT96/abstract.html — verbatim:
  > *"We determine what information about failures is necessary and sufficient to solve Consensus in asynchronous distributed systems subject to crash failures. In [ChTo91c], it is shown that ◇𝒲, a failure detector that provides surprisingly little information about which processes have crashed, is sufficient to solve Consensus in asynchronous systems with a majority of correct processes. In this paper, we prove that to solve Consensus, any failure detector has to provide at least as much information as ◇𝒲. Thus, ◇𝒲 is indeed the weakest failure detector for solving Consensus in asynchronous systems with a majority of correct processes."*
- **Assumption on the number of faulty processes: a majority of correct processes** (stated twice in the abstract above). The CT96 paper restates it as its own Theorem 6.2.5 / Corollary 6.2.6 with the majority condition made explicit (verbatim from the CT96 scan):
  - *"THEOREM 6.2.5 [CHANDRA ET AL. 1992]. If a failure detector 𝒟 can be used to solve Consensus in an asynchronous system, then 𝒟 ⊒ ◇𝒲0 in that system."*
  - *"COROLLARY 6.2.6. ◇𝒲0 is the weakest failure detector for solving Consensus in asynchronous systems with f < ⌈n/2⌉."*
  - with *"Let ◇𝒲0 be the 'weakest' failure detector in ◇𝒲. Roughly speaking, ◇𝒲0 is the failure detector that exhibits all the failure detector behaviors allowed by the properties that define ◇𝒲. More precisely, ◇𝒲0 consists of all the failure detector histories that satisfy weak completeness and eventual weak accuracy."*
- Note on notation: ◇𝒲0 here is what the later literature calls **Ω** (the leader-election / eventual-leader failure detector). CT96 itself flags the companion paper: *"A companion paper shows that one of the failure detectors introduced here is the weakest failure detector..."*
